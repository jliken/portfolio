Alongside my duties as Structures Co-Lead in endeavour, I decided to pursue a personal UKRA license to fly L1 high-powered rockets, in order to gain confidence in my own abilities to construct full-stack launch vehicles. The three rockets increased in impulse class: Estes A8 (Eos) to CTI 127G46 (DAE-I) to CTI 176H123 (DAE-II), demonstrating a solid understanding of rocket behaviour across different model and L1 regimes. The successful launch and recovery of all three rockets permitted my certification to launch personal L1 rockets.

Eos, which flew on an Estes A8 motor, was simply a model rocket kit build - the Estes Wizard. I tried to treat this rocket as I would any other, by simulating it in OpenRocket with post-manufacture masses to understand its expected flight profile. I used shredded iceberg lettuce as recovery wadding. Eos launched and recovered on the 16th November 2025, alongside the ASI-V rockets that I had supervised.

Daedalus 1 (DAE-I), which flew on a CTI G46 motor, was scratch-built with some personal innovations onboard. This rocket featured a removable motor mount and fin can, as well as a payload bay within the nosecone housing the Nano V1 altimeter. The production of a removable motor mount and fin can proved challenging, but could permit updates to motor sizing and fin profiles, as well as general serviceability. A full CAD model of this rocket was produced to aid in manufacture, as well as OpenRocket simulations to characterise expected flight behaviour. DAE-I launched and recovered successfully on the 31st May 2026, alongside five other L1 certification lights, a re-flight of ASI-Vb (Donatello), and Gemini's sustainer. All flight statistics measured by the onboard altimeter were in good agreement with simulated statistics.

Daedalus 2 (DAE-II), which flew on a CTI H123 motor, was an evolution of DAE-I. This rocket featured a removable motor mount (but not fin can) that was held in with four bolts rather than twelve, and a payload bay holding a camera, alarm, and Nano V1 altimeter. A removable fin can was deemed overly complex for the manufacture timeline, and the number of bolts was reduced to simplify stack integration. The payload aimed to capture onboard footage and flight data, as well as aid in recovery using the alarm. A full CAD model and numeroud OpenRocket simulations were produced for this rocket. DAE-II launched and recovered successfully on the 28th August 2026, alongside two other L1 certification flights and Gemini's 2-stage flight. Unfortunately, for undetermined reasons, the camera and altimeter independently failed to record any data during the flight.

By designing and manufacturing these rockets I have built a better understanding of flight stack integration and subsonic flight behaviour. Furthermore, I was able to plan and implement a number of personal innovations. My work towards my L1 certification will prove invaluable in my future personal rocketry projects, and possibly in my professional career.
