In nature we observe many symmetries in various physical laws. An example is rotational symmetry: if a given system is rotated by an arbitrary angle, it may still exhibit the same physical behaviour as before. By finding these symmetries, physicists can generalise the laws of physics to any system. However, there is also a lot of interest in broken symmetries - why do they exist? For example, time-symmetry is broken in our everyday perception of the universe: the past can affect the future, but the future can't affect the past. This is called "causal behaviour" - where a cause leads to an effect.

My master's thesis explored the fundamental nature of gravitational interactions, and why these interactions behave causally, despite the underlying physical laws having no time-dependence. Why do gravitational interactions propagate towards the future, causally, when our physical laws indicate that it shouldn't have a preference?

[Image illustrating point]

How can you imagine a gravitational interaction travelling towards the future, and acting on something? A good place to start is a black hole merger. As two black holes come close to each other, they rapidly orbit each other, spinning closer and closer until they combine into one black hole. This orbiting behaviour produces ripples in space, called gravitational waves. These waves travel through space at the speed of light, stretching and compressing space along their paths, eventually acting on something. The LIGO experiment in Washington and Louisiana monitors gravitational waves by tracking how an incredibly long laser beam distorts under the influence of these waves. And so, two black holes colliding on the other side of the galaxy can be tracked here on Earth, without ever seeing the process occur! 

[diagram showing the above]

You can think of this process as two black holes emitting energy, in the form of gravitational waves, towards the future to act on something. But this process isn't limited to enormous objects like black holes: everything with mass "emits" gravity towards everything else, all the way down to a particle level. And here lies the question: why do gravitational waves only travel to the future? And do we have a full answer to this question? No. The literature just assumes that these waves travel only towards the future.

My thesis proposed the possibility that all objects with mass emit gravitational waves towards the future and the past: displaying time-symmetry. But, our actual observations show that gravitational waves travel only towards the future. How do we get rid of the past-propagating waves? The physicists Richard Feynman and John Wheeler proposed a similar idea for electromagnetic objects, and their solution inspired mine. They suggested, in 1945, that some "boundary" in the future of the universe emits waves only towards the past, and these past-travelling waves cancel out with all of the other past-travelling waves, while boosting the future-travelling waves. That's a pretty exotic theory, if you ask me, but mathematically it checks out. Howevere, there's an elephant in the room: why does the boundary only emit waves towards the past? And what is this "boundary", physically?

[diagram trying to illustrate the above]

My theory went for a similar but opposite approach. I place a massive boundary at the start of the universe - the Big Bang - that emits gravitational waves only towards the future. These waves similarly cancel out all of the past-travelling waves, while boosting the future-travelling waves. Again, this works out mathematically (a very rigorous derivation that I won't provide here). Furthermore, you could naively say that the Big Bang was at the start of time, so there was no past for it to emit waves into, and that's why it only emits waves into the future. But I wasn't satisfied with that explanation. I wanted all objects with mass, including this massive boundary, to display time-symmetry.

[diagram showing the above point]

To solve this problem, I proposed the existence of a mirror universe. This universe is incredibly similar to our Universe, except it "lives" on the other side of the Big Bang: in the past. In this universe, gravitational waves appear to travel towards the past, instead of the future. This meant that my boundary, sat at the Big Bang, could emit gravitational waves towards the past and future. The past waves would "fix physics" in the mirror universe, and the future waves would "fix physics" in our universe. And this way, time symmetry is fundamental to all massive objects across the entire two-universe system!

[diagram]

My derived equations behind this theory simplify to be exactly the same equations used in literature, which assumes that gravitational waves travel only towards the future. Except there is no symmetry-breaking assumption occurring in my theory! Nature likes to deal in symmetries, so my explanation of gravitational wave behaviour through the use of symmetries is physically plausible.
